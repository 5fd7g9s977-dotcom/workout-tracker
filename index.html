Progression Guide

Principles and rules for building strength safely over time.

The Golden Rule of Progression

To continually build strength and endurance, you must systematically challenge your muscles over time—a concept known as Progressive Overload. Don’t guess how much weight you should be lifting today; use your previous logged sessions as the baseline to beat.

1. Form

Form is critical. Breaking form when lifting any weight risks injury. Ensure you are maintaining good form throughout all sets before adding more reps or more weight. If you are unable to maintain form, reduce reps or weight.

Ensure you are lifting through the full range of motion of the exercise. Quick, short lifts are not what we are looking for. We want fluid, well paced lifts that move through the entire range of motion for the exercise. Adjusting body position is important to achieve this.

Be sure to breathe. Generally speaking, exhale during the lift phase and inhale as you slowly lower the weight. Weight training, when done right, is as much a breathing exercise as it is a body movement exercise. If you find yourself short of breath or holding your breath, you may have too much weight or have lost your form. Proper breathing will help you achieve your lifting goals, especially when you are reaching your limits.

2. The Double Progression Method

The safest and most effective way to build strength is to increase reps before adding weight.

Phase 1: Build Reps First

Start with a weight you know you can lift for 10 reps and attempt to complete 3 sets of 10. Once you can complete 3 sets of 10 reps without breaking form, increase the number of reps to 15. You may not be able to complete all 3 sets with 15 reps initially. That’s ok. Once you can complete 3 sets of 15 reps, again, without breaking form, increase the number of reps to 20.

Phase 2: Step Up Weight Gradually

Only once you can complete 3 sets of 20 without breaking form, add weight. Increase the weight by the smallest available increment (2.5–5 lbs for upper body movements; 5–10 lbs for lower body movements) during your next session, and drop your target reps back to the bottom of the range, 10 reps.

Phase 3: Repeat the Cycle

Build your reps back up from 10-20 reps at the new, heavier weight before increasing resistance again.

3. Rating Session Difficulty

Use the difficulty selector on each exercise card to inform your progression strategy for the next workout:

  • Easy: Weight felt light with 3+ reps left in reserve. Candidate for an immediate weight or rep increase next time.
  • Moderate: Sweet spot for growth. Challenging with 1–2 clean reps in reserve. Hold weight steady and focus on adding reps next session.
  • Hard: Pushed to near-total failure or form began to degrade. Keep weight and reps constant until execution becomes smooth.

4. Overcoming Plateaus & Deloading

Strength gains are non-linear. If performance stalls at the same weight and rep count for 3 consecutive sessions:

  • Perform a Deload: Lower the weight by 10–15% for one workout and focus on speed, range of motion, and technique.
  • Rebuild: Return to your target working weight in the following session—you will frequently break through previous plateaus.
  • Optimize Recovery: Growth happens during rest. Ensure 7–9 hours of sleep, adequate hydration, and sufficient protein intake.

5. Progression for Cardio & Conditioning

Cardio exercises progress through duration, distance, and machine resistance rather than sets and reps:

  • Duration First: Add 2–5 minutes per session until reaching your target workout duration.
  • Resistance / Level Next: Increase machine resistance level or incline by 1 notch while maintaining target duration.
  • Distance & Pace: Aim to cover slightly more distance in the same time frame while maintaining steady breathing.
